# Answer Key — Quiz 6

**Q1 — C. Avoiding immune destruction.**
This was added in 2011 as an emerging hallmark, alongside reprogramming energy metabolism. The original six were:
1. self-sufficiency in growth signals
2. insensitivity to anti-growth signals
3. evading apoptosis
4. limitless replicative potential
5. sustained angiogenesis
6. tissue invasion and metastasis

*(Section 1)*

**Q2**
- (a) **Sustaining proliferative signalling** — RAS locked in its "on" state.
- (b) **Enabling replicative immortality** — telomerase reactivation.
- (c) **Evading growth suppressors** — loss of p16 frees CDK4/6 and Rb. It also weakens p53 via ARF loss, which relates to resisting cell death.
- (d) **Resisting cell death** — anti-apoptotic BCL2 is overexpressed.

*(Sections 1, 4, 5)*

**Q3 — B.**
PI3K converts PIP2 to PIP3, which activates AKT. PTEN removes that phosphate and switches the signal off, so losing PTEN keeps AKT on. Option D describes NF1 (a RAS-GAP); C describes MDM2. *(Section 3)*

**Q4 — Model answer.**
The CDKN2A locus encodes two proteins from **different first exons read in different reading frames** over shared downstream exons:
- **p16INK4a** inhibits CDK4/6, keeping Rb active (Rb pathway).
- **p14ARF** inhibits MDM2, protecting p53 (p53 pathway).

A homozygous deletion removes both proteins at once, so it disables two key tumour-suppressive circuits in a single event. *(Section 4)*

**Q5 — B.**
Hereditary cases already carry one hit in every cell, so one more somatic hit in any retinal cell is enough. That happens often enough to cause tumours in both eyes, and earlier. Sporadic cases need two independent hits in the same cell, so they are unilateral and later. *(Section 6)*

**Q6 — Model answer (any three).**
- A second point mutation or indel.
- A deletion of the wild-type allele.
- Loss of the chromosome or arm, possibly with reduplication of the mutant copy (copy-neutral LOH).
- Mitotic recombination (copy-neutral LOH extending to the telomere).
- **Promoter methylation silencing the wild-type allele — WGS alone cannot detect this.** It needs methylation assays, or RNA showing low expression.

*(Section 6)*

**Q7 — B.**
Strong oncogenic signalling in a normal cell can trigger a protective permanent growth arrest called senescence. Moles carrying BRAF V600E stop growing until further hits (e.g. CDKN2A loss) overcome the barrier. BRAF V600E is a genuine driver (A is wrong), but drivers alone are not sufficient for cancer. *(Worked example; Section 7)*

**Q8 — Model answer.**
KRAS and BRAF act in the same relay (RAS→RAF→MEK→ERK). Once one is activated, a second activating hit in the same pathway gives little extra advantage, and excessive signalling can even induce senescence. So tumours rarely carry both.

In cohort data, strong mutual exclusivity between genes suggests they act in the **same pathway** or serve the **same function** — a useful clue when interpreting less familiar genes. *(Section 3)*

**Q9 — C.**
dN/dS compares the rate of protein-changing (non-synonymous) mutations with silent (synonymous) ones, adjusted for expected rates. A value well above 1 means protein-changing mutations are being selected for, as expected for a driver. A value below 1 indicates purifying selection. *(Section 7)*

**Q10 — Model answer.**
The gene is most likely a **tumour suppressor**: truncations are scattered along its length rather than at hotspots, and LOH acts as the second hit (the 20/20 rule from Module 3).

Drugs are good at **blocking** an overactive protein but cannot easily **restore** a lost protein. Alternative strategies:
- **Synthetic lethality** (e.g. PARP inhibitors when BRCA1/2 is lost).
- Targeting the pathway **downstream** of the lost brake (e.g. PI3K/AKT/mTOR inhibitors after PTEN loss; CDK4/6 inhibitors when CDKN2A is lost and Rb is intact).

*(Sections 2–4)*
